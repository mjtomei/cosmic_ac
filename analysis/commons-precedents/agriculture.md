## The single best number

**US agriculture nearly tripled total factor productivity while its terms of
trade fell by two-thirds.** Computed from ERS Tables 1 and 1a (1948–2023):
TFP **×2.84**, output **×3.10**, inputs **+9%** — and output price ÷ input
price fell from **2.605 to 0.916, i.e. to 35.2% of its 1948 level**. That is
Cochrane's treadmill in one row, from official data, and the cleanest possible
statement that the gains did not stay on the farm. Gardner's independent
restatement: real prices received by farmers fell ~1%/yr across 1900–2000.
**Citation hygiene: do not print a page or chapter cite for Cochrane's
treadmill — the 1958 book is not digitized anywhere reachable. Cite
"Cochrane (1958)" or via Levins & Cochrane (1996).**

## The institutional commons, and its decline

Morrill (1862) / Hatch (1887) / Smith-Lever (1914) — publicly funded research
with open dissemination and free extension. Decline, confirmed exactly:
**peak $7.64B (2002) → $5.16B (2019), constant 2019 dollars, BRDPI-deflated =
−32.5%** (Nelson & Fuglie, *Amber Waves*, 2022-06-06; independently recomputed
at −32.8% in 2022 dollars). ERS's own framing: US public spending is now
*"roughly where it was in 1970"* in real terms ($5,468M in 1970 vs $5,450M in
2019). Public/private: private **first exceeded public in the late 1970s, fell
behind again 1992–2002, and has led continuously since 2003** (73.1% by 2013).
*(The "since 2010" shorthand is wrong; the private input-industry series ends
in 2014 — do not compute a private share after that.)* China crossed the US
around 2009 and reached ~$10.4B against the US's ~$4.8B by 2015–18 [MEDIUM-HIGH
— crossing year read off the ERS chart, not stated in text].

Public breeding capacity now: **278 public programs in 44 states, mean 2.78
FTE, median annual operating budget $150,000**, ~80% under $400,000, nearly
half of program leaders near retirement (Coe, Evans, Gasic & Main, *Crop
Science* 60(5), 2020). Shelton & Tracy (*Crop Science* 57(4), 2017) document
germplasm access impeded by restrictive licensing **inside the public sector**
— the enclosure operating within the commons itself.

## ⚠️ Olson correction — the shorthand is backwards, and the truth is sharper

Olson's actual text: *"The most striking fact about the political organization
of farmers in the United States is that there has been so little. Farmers have
not on the whole been well organized"* (p. 148). The Grange, the Alliances,
Greenback, Free Silver and Populism **all died within a few years**. The Farm
Bureau was the exception *precisely because it was not spontaneous*: **"For the
Farm Bureau was created by the government"** — via the Smith-Lever county-agent
system, sustained by selective incentives (co-ops, member-restricted mutual
insurance).

**So the extension system built the farm lobby.** This corrects the tidy
"Populism → New Deal redistribution" arc: the populist movements failed, and
the durable political vehicle was manufactured by the commons institution
itself, then later captured the subsidy apparatus. The commons generated the
constituency that enclosed it. That is a better story for the paper than the
clean one, and a darker one.
*(Do not merge Gardner's JPE 1987 into this — it is a Becker-style
efficient-redistribution model, not a collective-action model.)*

**Best citation for productivity squeeze → political demand for redistribution:**
Anderson, Rausser & Swinnen, "Political Economy of Public Policies," *JEL*
51(2): 423–477 (2013), DOI 10.1257/jel.51.2.423, §3.2.1: *"This income gap
creates incentives for agricultural entities to demand—and politicians to
supply—policies that redistribute income in order to reduce that income gap."*
§3.2.3 gives the Olsonian mechanism correctly: organizing costs *fall* as
agriculture's employment share declines. ⚠️ Honest flag: the pattern has
**partially reversed since ~1985** (EU RRA 77% in the 1980s → 11% in 2005–10).
State as a historical pattern with an unresolved recent phase, not a law.

## ⭐ The most transferable finding: why OSSI abandoned copyleft

**Not enforceability — propagation.** OSSI's own FAQ says *"We believe the
Pledge to be legally enforceable."* The binding constraints were two:

1. **No automatic default right to invert.** Montenegro de Wit, *J. Peasant
   Studies* 46(1): 44–79 (DOI 10.1080/03066150.2017.1383395): *"unlike software
   code, for which the code-writer automatically receives copyright protection,
   the creation of novel genetic sequences does not immediately grant the plant
   breeder an analogous right."* Copyleft is a judo move on an automatically
   granted exclusive right. Without the right, there is nothing to invert.
2. **No transmission channel wide enough to carry the licence.** Luby &
   Goldman, *PLOS Biology* 14(4):e1002441 (2016): the copyleft licence
   *"proved too cumbersome for practical use."* Montenegro de Wit: *"figuring
   out how to affix eight-page contracts onto 3 × 4 inch packets of seeds…
   a licence would most likely fail to propagate for more than a few
   generations… gone would be the likelihood of going 'viral'."*

**Why this matters far beyond agriculture.** For a paper arguing that open
weights are a durable, self-propagating commons, this is a published account of
exactly when copyleft fails to bootstrap — and **the copyright status of model
weights is itself unsettled**, which puts weights closer to seed than to source
code on requirement (1). If there is no automatic exclusive right in weights,
then viral licensing cannot manufacture the commons, and its durability has to
rest on irreversibility and norms instead. That is a precise, non-obvious,
already-published lesson to build on rather than a novelty claim.
Prior art running the analogy the other way (software → agriculture):
Kloppenburg, "Impeding Dispossession, Enabling Repossession," *J. Agrarian
Change* 10(3): 367–388 (2010), proposing a "General Public Licence for Plant
Germplasm," citing Stallman, Linux and CAMBIA BiOS.

**Two verified negatives, both correcting plausible-sounding attributions:**
Boyle 2003 (*"The Second Enclosure Movement"*) **does not discuss agriculture,
seeds or plant genetics at all** — full-text grep; every enclosure reference is
English land or human genomics. Boyle supplies the frame; **Kloppenburg is the
author who applies it to seed** (and quotes the c.1821 enclosure verse *from*
Boyle). **Hess & Ostrom 2007 has no agricultural chapter** — confirmed from the
complete Crossref TOC; all twelve chapters are scholarly communication, digital
libraries, open access and IP. The volume does contain Schweik on FOSS as a
framework for commons in science, so the FOSS→science bridge exists; the
agriculture leg does not.

## ⭐ Matthew's extension (2026-08-08): weights are closer to seed than to software

His formulation: *"open weights may end up being more similar to seed than
software because you can make slight alterations or train a new model with the
same capabilities once you know they exist and some details about training. So
protections are very difficult to enforce there too."*

Three consequences, in ascending order of usefulness to the paper:

1. **It generalizes the paper's own "teaches its own manufacture" claim from
   open to CLOSED models.** A served closed model reveals that its capability
   level is achievable, and knowing a thing is possible (plus roughly how) is a
   large fraction of the work. So the open/closed distinction protects less than
   assumed, and the protection fails at the *capability*, not at the weights —
   the weights were never the valuable object.
2. **It is Arrow 1962 landing harder here than where we planned to use it:**
   "the very use of the information in any productive way is bound to reveal it,
   at least in part." Serving a model is using the information productively;
   revelation is the business model, not a leak.
3. **It makes the seed analogy predictive rather than decorative**, via the
   structural detail verified above. Plant Variety Protection carries a
   **breeder's exemption** — a protected variety may be used to breed a new one
   — which is exactly the breed-around route. **Utility patents carry no such
   exemption**, and the verified concentration data tracks that difference
   precisely: three firms hold **86–90% of the IP across GM crops** (utility
   patents apply) against **27–35% across non-GM** (largely they don't).
   Enclosure concentrated wherever a strong, non-workaroundable right existed.
   **Prediction for weights:** copyright, if it attaches at all, covers a
   specific artifact rather than the capability — the breeder's-exemption case;
   training-method patents are narrow and designable-around; trade secret is the
   labs' actual instrument and fails on public deployment (hence the thinning
   tech reports). Capability therefore looks like the *non-GM* case: weak right,
   low enclosure. The durable question is not whether weights leak but whether
   any right covers capability itself.

**Honest counters to carry:** reimplementation compresses rather than eliminates
cost (DeepSeek is the datapoint on how much compression is available); labs have
deliberately gone dark on the training details the argument needs, which is
trade secret working as designed. ~~and distillation is being contested legally
rather than conceded~~ — **CORRECTED 2026-08-08: that claim was wrong. No AI
model provider anywhere has sued over model outputs, distillation, or a
no-competing-model API clause. See `weights-ip.md`; the absence of litigation
strengthens the extension rather than qualifying it.** **Verification in flight
2026-08-08** on the hinge — whether any
automatic exclusive right attaches to weights (see the flag below); if none
does, the commons cannot be manufactured by licence and its durability rests on
irreversibility and norms instead.

## Enclosure: where it happened, and the honest limits

**Enclosure is concentrated exactly where the IP is strongest** — the sharpest
finding here, and the most auditable, since both series are government
registries (17,208 USPTO utility patents 1976–2021; 8,407 USDA PVP certificates
2001–21). Bayer + Corteva + Syngenta hold **95% of corn utility patents and 98%
of corn PVP certificates**, **86%/90% across all GM crops**, but only
**35%/27% across non-GM crops**. CR4: corn 83.4, soybeans 78.1, cotton 93.6
(2018–20), against corn CR4 of 57.1 in 2000–03. Seed prices 1990–2020: **+270%
average, +463% for predominantly-GM crops**, against +56% commodity inflation.

⚠️ **Three flags that must travel with those numbers.**
- **"The Big Four control 60% of seeds" is wrong three ways.** ETC's own current
  data (*Food Barons 2022*) gives **top-4 = 51% of the commercial seed market**
  (top-6 = 58%); 60% is their older 2018 figure. And ETC states in the same
  report that **farmer-controlled seed networks remain 80–90% of all seed
  planted globally**, so the Big Four's share of the world's seed is nearer
  5–10%. For one robust number use **agrochemicals at 62.3%**, which USDA ERS
  independently corroborates.
- **Concentration levels are uncontested; harm from them is not.** ERS itself
  reports the OECD 2018 study finding *no significant correlation between market
  concentration and seed prices* for field crops, and attaches a disclaimer to
  its own price series. Deconinck, *Annual Review of Resource Economics* 12
  (2020), DOI 10.1146/annurev-resource-102319-100751: *"Although growing
  concentration might in theory lead to higher prices and less innovation,
  evidence on this is currently limited."* **Do not present rising CR4 → rising
  seed prices as a demonstrated causal chain.**
- Deal-value hygiene: **Bayer-Monsanto $66B is the aggregate including net debt;
  ~$63B is the equity consideration** (commonly stated backwards; $128.00/share,
  44% premium, closed 2018-06-07). **Dow-DuPont has no deal value** — all-stock
  merger of equals; any "$130B" is combined market cap, not a price, so do not
  tabulate it beside ChemChina-Syngenta ($43B).
