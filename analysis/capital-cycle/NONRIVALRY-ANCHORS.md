# Non-rivalry argument: anchors and prior art

2026-08-08 verification pass (Opus agent, primary PDFs fetched and text-extracted
rather than snippet-searched). Supports Matthew's 2026-08-07 argument: previous
buildouts socialized capital only through **crash and dispossession** because the
asset was rival and appropriable; the AI buildout's durable output is **non-rival
and copyable**, so it can socialize **without transfer of ownership and without a
crash** — copying is not dispossession. Consequence: the pop, if it comes, is
caused by the technology *working* rather than by demand failing.

## Verified anchors (verbatim from primary sources)

**Arrow 1962** — Kenneth J. Arrow, "Economic Welfare and the Allocation of
Resources for Invention," in R. R. Nelson (ed.), *The Rate and Direction of
Inventive Activity*, NBER/Princeton UP, 1962, pp. 609–626. DOI
10.1515/9781400879762-024. PDF: nber.org/chapters/c2144.pdf. Three claims, all
in his words:
- non-rival: "Any one purchaser can destroy th[e] monopoly, since he can
  reproduce the information at little or no cost" (p. 614).
- inappropriable: **"no amount of legal protection can make a thoroughly
  appropriable commodity of something so intangible as information. The very use
  of the information in any productive way is bound to reveal it, at least in
  part"** (p. 615).
- therefore underinvestment: "we expect a free enterprise economy to underinvest
  in invention and research… because the product can be appropriated only to a
  limited extent" (p. 618); and "precisely to the extent that it is successful,
  there is an underutilization of the information" (p. 616).
**Pairing note:** the section already quotes Arrow 1969 for the pressure-toward-
collective-action point. Arrow 1962 supplies the reason the value cannot be
captured. Same author, both halves — state the pair when drafting.

**Romer 1990** — "Endogenous Technological Change," *JPE* 98(5, Pt 2),
pp. S71–S102, DOI 10.1086/261725. Best sentence for weights (p. S75): "the cost
of replicating it with a drafter, a photocopier, or a disk drive is trivial
compared to the cost of creating the design in the first place." Also: technology
is "a nonrival, partially excludable good."

**Teece 1986** — "Profiting from technological innovation: Implications for
integration, collaboration, licensing and public policy," *Research Policy*
15(6), pp. 285–305, DOI 10.1016/0048-7333(86)90027-2. Innovators often fail to
capture returns; profits accrue to owners of specialized complementary assets.
(Note the full subtitle; the short title is an abbreviation.)

**Nordhaus 2004** — the 2.2% capture figure is verified verbatim on p. 22 of NBER
WP 10433 ("innovators are able to capture about 2.2 percent of the total social
surplus from innovation"; range 1.3–3.3%). **The published abstract says only "a
minuscule fraction" — cite the working paper, not the abstract.**

**Bresnahan & Trajtenberg 1995** — "General Purpose Technologies: 'Engines of
Growth?'", *J. Econometrics* 65(1). "Innovational complementarities" /
dual-inducement: GPT suppliers and application sectors each under-invest because
neither internalizes the other's returns. **The paper's collective-action
mechanism has a thirty-year-old name — build on it, per the no-novelty rule.**

## Prior art, by part of the argument

**(a) "Open weights commoditize capability and undermine the returns the capex
requires" — NOT NOVEL, published repeatedly. Cite into it.**
Mark Surman (Mozilla Foundation president), "Open source could pop the AI bubble
— and soon," *FT* op-ed, 2025-12-17 [PAYWALLED — re-verify before citing];
Glyn Moody, *Techdirt*, 2025-12-16; Dean Ball on Kimi K3 (2026-07, same
mechanism, concludes capability slowdown not bust; no long-form original found);
Horace Dediu's commoditization thesis, **named and rebutted directly by Ben
Thompson, "Agents Over Bubbles," Stratechery 2026** — so it is an established
position with an established counter. Journal version: Ciarrocchi, "The Free
Lunch Dilemma," *California Management Review*, 2026-02. Quantified premise:
Nagle & Yue (MIT SMR, 2025). Ur-text: Sernau, "We Have No Moat," leaked via
SemiAnalysis 2023 (trade press only, no academic citations).

**(b) Appropriability of AI capability — a real academic literature exists.**
- **Azoulay, Krieger & Nagaraj, "Old Moats for New Models: Openness, Control, and
  Competition in Generative AI," NBER WP 32474 (2024)** — Teece applied to
  generative AI, in print. SAME on appropriability, DIFFERENT conclusion (they
  predict concentration). ⚠️ **Do not claim that Teece has not been applied to
  open-weight foundation models — this paper is exactly that.**
- Jones & Tonetti, "Nonrivalry and the Economics of Data," *AER* 110(9), 2020 —
  formal model of a nonrival digital input that firms hoard. **The weights
  analogue does not appear to exist: a real, narrow gap.**
- **Korinek & Vipra, "Concentrating Intelligence," *Economic Policy* 2024** —
  frontier layer has natural-monopoly properties. The serious opposing case;
  cite as such.
- Athey & Scott Morton, NBER WP 34444 (2025); Acemoglu & Restrepo NBER 32536
  (rent dissipation offsets 60–90% of automation's productivity gains).
- **Borri, Liu & Tsyvinski, "AI Premium," arXiv:2606.30583** — the AI equity
  premium loads on closed-source models and paying users, **not** on open-weight
  use. Quantitative open/closed appropriability split.
- **Björkegren, "Market Beliefs about Open vs. Closed AI," arXiv:2512.14969** —
  long-term bond yields "shift in opposite directions following the introduction
  of open versus closed models." A clean natural experiment on the premise.

**(c) The historical INVERSION (crash-and-dispossession vs copy-without-
dispossession) — NOTHING CLOSE FOUND.** Kedrosky & Thompson (2025-10-02) make
the railroad/telecom comparison in the *conventional* direction, and Kedrosky's
distinguishing feature for AI is **asset lifetime/depreciation** — adjacent to
our recognition-half-life work, but not non-rivalry. Ed Zitron (2026-08)
*explicitly rejects* the commoditization route ("not from open source… not from
anywhere") — pure demand-shortfall; the foil. Bain is also pure demand-gap.
**Recommended framing: a contrast drawn on top of three published legs —
Perez/Janeway/Hobart-Huber (bubbles leave a legacy), Arrow/Romer/Jones-Tonetti
(the legacy is non-rival this time), Nordhaus (innovators capture 2.2% anyway,
so socialization is the historical norm). Zero novelty claims.** ⚠️ The specific
sentence in Perez or Janeway asserting *the crash is the mechanism* by which
infrastructure becomes cheap was NOT verifiable (carlotaperez.org 406s, Cambridge
page 500s) — verify before it becomes load-bearing.

**(d) The counter-argument — excludability migrates from weights to compute.**
- **Toby Ord, "Inference Scaling Reshapes AI Governance," arXiv:2503.05705 (2025)
  — the direct rebuttal, as its headline claim:** scaling inference-at-deployment
  "would lower the importance of open-weight models"; open models needing vast
  user-side inference are "much less attractive… than models of equivalent
  capability that were entirely pre-trained." Engage this explicitly.
- **Sastry, Heim, Belfield, Anderljung, Brundage et al. (19 authors incl. Bengio,
  Coyle), "Computing Power and the Governance of AI," arXiv:2402.08797** —
  compute is "detectable, excludable, and quantifiable… produced via an extremely
  concentrated supply chain," explicitly contrasted with models as "easily
  shareable, non-rivalrous intangible goods." **Concedes our non-rivalry premise
  and relocates the rivalry to silicon** — the exact vocabulary of the counter.
- Supporting the counter: Snell et al. arXiv:2408.03314 (test-time compute
  substitutes for parameters); ARC Prize o3 at $4,560/task.

**Where the counter is weakest — the most useful finding.** Compute-excludability
**decays as fast as it is built**: distillation puts o1-mini-class reasoning into
a 32B single-GPU model (DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-32B: AIME-2024 72.6%, MATH-500
94.3% vs o1-mini 63.6%/90.0%), and Epoch measures price-per-capability falling
**9×–900×/year** (~40×/year for GPT-4-level GPQA). The appropriable asset is a
wasting one — which supports the paper's thesis better than either pole.

**Open-weight frontier lag — the numbers moved sharply, use the recent ones.**
Epoch Nov 2024: ~1 year (training-compute lag ~15 months). Epoch 2025-10-30:
**3.5 months** (90% CI 1.1–5.3), "sometimes even closing completely." Epoch
2026-05-29: **~4 months average since January 2026**. Stanford HAI AI Index 2025:
Chatbot Arena closed-vs-open gap 8.04% (Jan 2024) → **1.70%** (Feb 2025). The
frontier-lag objection is much weaker than when it was first made.

## "Capability improves faster than Moore's law" — verified 2026-08-08, weaker than the rhetoric

Checked at Matthew's request. **Defensible in exactly one form**, and the honest
version is much more modest than the phrase suggests.

**The legitimate pairing is algorithmic FLOP-efficiency × hardware FLOP/$.**
These are genuinely independent: efficiency is measured in FLOP-to-reach-fixed-
performance, which is hardware-independent by construction. Hernandez & Brown
(OpenAI, arXiv:2005.04305, 2020) state the multiplicative claim themselves —
the two "multiply and can be on a similar scale over meaningful horizons" —
and note Moore's law alone would have given 11× over their window against a
measured 44×.

**Illegitimate pairings, all of which double-count:**
- Training-compute growth (5.3×/yr 2010–2024, 4.2×/yr since 2018; Epoch) ×
  hardware. Training compute in FLOP **already contains** hardware
  price-performance, and since hardware supplies only ~1.37×/yr, **~3.5–3.9×/yr
  of that headline is spending growth, not technology.** Never use this form.
- Price-per-capability × the others. (C) is *already the compound*.
- Amodei's "~4×/yr algorithmic" bundles hardware and FLOP-utilization into the
  algorithmic term (Byrnes).

**⚠️ The denominator correction — do not use the "Moore's law is dead" move.**
Epoch measures ML-hardware price-performance at **37%/yr, doubling every 2.2
years**, and finds it "approximately follows general computational performance"
at ~2.3-year doubling. What died was Dennard scaling and single-thread
performance, **not FLOP/$**. The fair comparison is against a still-live
~1.37×/yr. (An earlier claim in this conversation that the bar is lower because
Moore's law slowed was WRONG for this denominator.)

**Numbers.** Hardware ~1.37×/yr × algorithmic ~1.75×/yr (Hernandez & Brown,
vision) to ~2.8×/yr (Ho/Erdil/Besiroglu et al., arXiv:2403.05812, language,
8-month halving, **95% CI 5–14 months** — a 3× spread — on two *perplexity*
benchmarks, not capability). Net: **faster than Moore's law by roughly
1.6×–2×/yr of extra multiplier.** Real and compounding; not an order-of-magnitude
gulf.

**⚠️ The counter we should cite ourselves, per the honest-flags convention.**
**Gundlach, Fogelson, Lynch, Trisovic, Rosenfeld, Sandhu & Thompson (MIT,
arXiv:2511.21622, Nov 2025)**: prior work claimed algorithms improved training
efficiency **22,000× over 2012–2023**; their ablations on the actual named
innovations account for **under 10×**, and **under 100×** with everything in the
literature added. Their resolution is that efficiency gains are **scale- and
reference-dependent**; extrapolating via scaling laws gives 6,930× (~2.16×/yr),
**dominated by two discrete architectural events (LSTM→Transformer, Chinchilla
rebalancing)** rather than smooth incremental innovation. Extrapolating the
headline rate forward assumes more such events arrive on schedule. Epoch's own
Gradient Updates concedes much of this (~2.23×/yr at frontier scale,
"substantially lower than the commonly-cited estimate," and much of "software
progress" may be **data quality**, which is bought). Byrnes puts core
learning-algorithm efficiency at ~10× total since 2018 (~30%/yr) — *below*
Moore's law — with data the single largest factor in his decomposition.

**Price-per-capability (verified exactly as we had it, but it is a market series
not a technology series).** Epoch: **9×–900×/yr across six benchmarks, ~40×/yr
for GPT-4-level GPQA Diamond**; concrete anchor $37.50/M tokens (GPT-4-0314,
Mar 2023) → $0.18/M (Gemini 2.0 Flash, Feb 2025) ≈ 208× in two years. Epoch's
caveats: the fastest drops are recent and may not persist; the 900× end is a
**benchmark-saturation artifact**; these are **posted prices**, reflecting
competitive strategy and loss-leading, not cost.

**METR (arXiv:2503.14499)** — 7-month doubling of the 50%-success time horizon,
but use as directional colour only: METR's own 2026-01-22 clarification says CIs
span **~2× in each direction** ("no idea whether Claude's 'true' time horizon is
3.5h or 6.5h"), horizons vary **40–100× across domains**, and the metric is
serial human labour replaceable at 50% success. Later analysis of Time Horizon
1.1 suggests ~3.5-month doubling for 2024–25 models. **No Moore's-law-comparable
units exist** — any "faster than Moore's law" built on METR is rhetorical.

**Implication for the wanting/absorption argument.** The right metric for "when
does local hardware clear the bar" is exactly the legitimate pairing (efficiency
× hardware), so the argument is properly served — at ~3×/yr compounding rather
than an order of magnitude. **New complication worth carrying:** if roughly
two-thirds of language-model progress came from scale, and data is the largest
single factor in Byrnes's decomposition, then progress is substantially
*purchased*. A capital-cycle break would therefore slow the very commoditization
that the endogenous-pop argument says causes the break — a negative feedback the
argument should acknowledge rather than a clean runaway.
