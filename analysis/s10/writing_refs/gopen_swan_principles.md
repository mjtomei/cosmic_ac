# Gopen & Swan — reader-expectation principles (distilled)

A checklist synthesised from George D. Gopen & Judith A. Swan, "The Science
of Scientific Writing," *American Scientist* 78 (1990). This is our own
distillation for priming the prose generator — not the article text.
Source PDF fetched 2026-09-01 (not committed; copyright Sigma Xi).

Core premise: readers don't just read, they *interpret*, and they make
interpretive decisions from *structural* cues. Writing is judged by whether
the majority of readers recover the writer's intended meaning — so put each
piece of information where readers already expect to find it.

1. **Subject–verb proximity.** Follow a grammatical subject as soon as
   possible with its verb. Readers hold everything between subject and verb
   as a mere interruption of lesser importance; a long gap buries whatever
   sits in it. (Long sentences are fine; interrupted ones are not.)
2. **One unit, one point.** Every unit of discourse — sentence, paragraph,
   section — should do a single job and make a single point. If it makes
   two, split it.
3. **The stress position.** Readers give natural emphasis to material at the
   *end* of a sentence (at the point of syntactic closure). Put the
   information you want emphasised there — "work up to the broccoli, don't
   start with the strawberry shortcake." A colon or semicolon creates a
   secondary stress position, so a long sentence can carry several emphases
   if each clause reaches closure before the next.
4. **The topic position.** The *beginning* of a sentence (the topic
   position) tells the reader whose story this is and links backward. Start
   with material that connects to what came before; don't open on new,
   unlinked information.
5. **Old before new.** Begin sentences with familiar/old information (the
   link) and end with the new (the emphasis). This is what makes a
   paragraph flow rather than lurch: each sentence's topic picks up the
   prior sentence's stress.
6. **Provide context before demanding interpretation.** Don't ask the reader
   to hold new facts before you've told them why they matter or where they
   fit.
7. **Match structural weight to substantive weight.** Put the ideas that
   deserve emphasis in the positions that carry emphasis (stress), and
   demote genuine asides out of subject–verb interruptions and stress slots
   — or cut them.
8. **Locate the action in the verb, the agent in the subject.** Prefer real
   verbs over nominalisations; say who did what.
9. **Diagnose by reading structurally.** When a passage is hard, don't
   assume it's the jargon — check for a subject–verb gap, a wasted stress
   position, a topic position that fails to link, or a unit trying to make
   two points.

Applying these is *substantive*, not cosmetic: Gopen & Swan's claim is that
improving the structure improves the thought, because it forces the writer
to decide what actually deserves emphasis and how ideas connect.
