# S10 prior art — detector studies of legislative speech

Surfaced 2026-08-09 by the AI-policy scan. **Both comparators verified against
primary sources 2026-08-11** (Pimlico earlier the same day). Corrections below
are load-bearing — several figures we had been repeating do not mean what we
took them to mean.

## The two comparators

| | Rice | Pimlico Journal |
|---|---|---|
| Corpus | Australian federal Hansard, 124,734 speeches (64,642 post-ChatGPT scored) | UK House of Commons, 2007–2025 |
| Instrument | Binoculars + Fast-DetectGPT + an LLM scoring pass (0–10) + per-MP stylometry | z-scores on subjectively chosen terms, volume-normalised |
| Result | **No significant post-ChatGPT increase** | suspects AI use; **no prevalence estimate given** |
| Reported FPR | ~8% at ≥8/10, n=50 (see below) | not reported |

**Rice, verified 2026-08-11.** Ben Rice, "Hunting for ChatGPT in Parliament
(and mostly not finding it)", *Rice On Paper* (Substack), **11 May 2026**.
Code at `github.com/benjames-stack/hansard-ai-detection` (MIT, single squashed
commit, **tooling only — no results, no numbers**). Every quantitative finding
exists solely in the blog post. Not peer-reviewed, no CIs, no p-values;
author's own disclaimer: "I am not a data-scientist, so please challenge me if
you think I'm wrong." Corpus is Commonwealth only (House and Senate), 2018–,
≥200 words, from checkhansard.com.au.

Four corrections to how we had been characterising it:

1. **The "FPR exceeds the detection rate" line does not hold as stated, and we
   repeated it.** The 8% is measured at the "highly likely AI" threshold
   (≥8/10); the 7.1% corpus rate is the "possibly AI" flag rate (≥6/10). His
   own pipeline computes both separately. Two thresholds, not one comparison.
2. **The 8% is n=50** — 4 of 50 pre-ChatGPT speeches, Wilson CI
   **[3.2%, 18.8%]**. Paired sensitivity was 20% (10/50), CI [11.2%, 33.0%].
3. **It characterises the wrong judge.** The post says only "the same scoring
   system", but the repo's `research_ai_calibrate.py` hardcodes
   `JUDGE_MODEL = "claude-haiku-4-5"`, while the headline full-corpus run used
   Sonnet 4.6. The 8% almost certainly describes the Haiku-class judge.
4. **He does not claim evidence of absence — he claims his detector is
   blind.** His own calibration script prints "✗ Detector BLIND" below 0.3
   sensitivity, and his measured 20% falls in that branch. In his words: "It is
   inconceivable that only three or four federal politicians have used AI to
   draft a speech in the past three years", and "At no setting can I draw a
   clean line between 'this was written by a person' and 'this was generated by
   an LLM' in parliamentary register." **Citing Rice as showing "no AI in
   Hansard" would misrepresent him.**

Two arithmetic slips of his, noted so we do not inherit them: "roughly one in
13,000" for 7/64,642 (it is 1 in 9,235), and "five different AI detection
tools" in prose against four in the repo. His stylometric arm (257 MPs, 162
flagged) he discards himself as confounded by the May 2022 election.

**Pimlico, verified 2026-08-11.** "MPs are almost certainly using ChatGPT to
generate Commons speeches", by the pseudonymous **"Apple Tokamak"**, Pimlico
Journal, 1 September 2025. Pimlico is a **Substack-based online magazine**
founded August 2023, not peer-reviewed and with no academic editorial board —
cite it as such. Search terms were chosen subjectively from "articles and
online discussions" plus the author's own experience of ChatGPT output.

Three corrections to how we had been characterising it:

1. **It reports no prevalence figure at all** — no fraction, no count. Framing
   it as a study that "detected an increase" overstates it; it presents word
   -frequency spikes and infers likely AI use.
2. **The author hedges explicitly**, and about the same secular trend that
   demoted our own arm: he attributes a rise since 2007 to "LinkedIn-ification"
   and writes that he "would be rather less confident in stating that the
   increase in usage since 2022 is because of AI". Of three speeches examined
   closely: "there is no definitive proof that any of these three speeches made
   use of AI."
3. **Press coverage is far more confident than the article** (Telegraph,
   11 Sep 2025). Cite the article, never the coverage.

**No formal trend control** — no placebo run on a pre-LLM window where the true
effect is zero. Same structural defect as our Kobak arm. One thing remains
unresolved and should be pinned before we characterise the method in print:
whether the z-score baseline is the full 2007–2025 series or a pre-2022 window.
If the former, any monotonically rising word mechanically produces large
terminal-year z-scores, effect or not — worse than merely lacking a placebo.

Neither used a calibrated commercial detector. Both predate S10 on chambers
that are in our corpus, so both must be engaged with directly.

## How S10 answers Rice

Not by impugning the detectors. Binoculars (Hans et al. 2024) and
Fast-DetectGPT (Bao et al. 2024) are published methods, and dismissing them
would not survive review.

**The argument is about sensitivity, not specificity.** Our earlier version of
this section rested on his FPR-exceeds-detection-rate line, which crosses
thresholds and does not hold (above). The durable answer is that his
instruments have very high false-negative rates on precisely this register.
On peer-review benchmarks reproduced in the Pangram 4 technical report
(arXiv:2607.27183), at 1% FPR both Binoculars and Fast-DetectGPT reach ~6%
true-positive rate — FNR above 90% — where a calibrated commercial detector
holds above 95%. On the harder Saha subset Binoculars runs 14–22% TPR. Formal
institutional prose is where these methods fail worst, and Hansard is that
genre. Rice reaches the same conclusion from his own calibration; we can cite
him agreeing rather than set him up as an opponent.

Note also that he and we are not measuring the same thing: his flag rate is
per-speech over a whole corpus with no chamber-level control window, while S10
buys a pre-AI control per chamber and reports prevalence against it.

S10's corresponding number is measured, not assumed: **423/423 specificity**
across four chambers, plus 2,400 pre-LLM segments in the Opus band screen
with zero flags, and every new chamber in the expansion buys its own 60-segment
pre-AI control rather than borrowing one. A chamber's false-positive rate
depends on its own editorial register, so specificity is not transferable.

## Why Pimlico's agreement is not corroboration

Pimlico's z-score method is the same family as S10's Kobak-lexicon arm, which
**we demoted from inferential to descriptive** after an in-time placebo showed
it carries no trend control (`in_time_placebo.py`). A method that agrees with
us while sharing the defect we found in our own version of it is weak support.
Treat it as a third result to explain, not as a second vote.

## The complication both studies run into

S10's permeation result — human speech drifting toward LLM register
independent of drafting — predicts exactly this split:

- **lexical z-score methods fire** on a real effect that is not machine
  drafting (Pimlico's positive)
- **detector methods get ambiguous**, because the human baseline is itself
  moving toward the thing being detected (part of Rice's null)

So the two prior studies may both be measuring something real and different.
The honest framing is not "we are right and Rice is wrong" but that
prevalence and permeation are separate quantities requiring separate
instruments, which is why S10 runs a calibrated detector for the first and
detector-independent tests for the second.

## Also flagged: transcript provenance

Several chambers now use AI/ASR in producing the record itself — reported for
NSW and WA Hansard, an approved Oireachtas use case for AI-drafted Journal
proceedings, and plenary transcription as the most common parliamentary AI
application in the EU (27 of 34 chambers). This confounds *any* detector study
of Hansard, prior or ours.

Measured locally rather than taken on report (`transcript_regime_check.py`):
NSW and WA are flat across 2006–2026 on sentence segmentation and contraction
density, so whatever they procured has not visibly changed the text.
**Tasmania is the chamber that actually moved** — contraction density 3.4 →
15.9 per 1,000 words across the windows, +364% — and its step falls between
the control and prevalence windows, so no pre-AI text exists in the current
regime to calibrate against. The corpus evidence and the procurement reports
point at different chambers.

## External false-negative rates, for the bypass comparison (§4.9)

Assembled 2026-08-11 so that our induced 8.5% has published context. **Labels
matter: ours is a false *negative* rate, Rice's ~8% is a false *positive*
rate, and the numeric coincidence is meaningless.**

| source | rate | quantity |
|---|---|---|
| Pangram 4 report, clean | 0.34% | FNR, known-AI |
| Pangram 4 report, 13 humanizer services | 2.31% | FNR under commercial evasion |
| Pangram 4 report, BLADER de-AI agent | 0.43% | FNR under agentic evasion |
| Pangram 4 report, Perkins benchmark | 2.86% | FNR, doc-level adversarial |
| Originality.ai, Perkins | 9.09% | FNR, doc-level adversarial |
| GPTZero / Originality.ai, humanizer split | 55.7% / 71.5% | FNR at 1% FPR |
| Binoculars / Fast-DetectGPT, peer review | ~93.8% | FNR at 1% FPR |

All Pangram rows are **vendor-reported on undisclosed test data** and must be
cited as such. The independent evaluations point the same way but harder:
RAID (Dugan et al., ACL 2024) finds detectors "easily fooled by adversarial
attacks" across 11 attack types, and Weber-Wulff et al. (2023, *Int. J.
Educational Integrity*) finds a systematic bias toward classifying output as
human-written, worsened by obfuscation. Our 8.5% sits above every vendor
adversarial figure and far below the legacy-detector ones.
