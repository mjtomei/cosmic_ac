## Arc 1 — The democratization of counsel

A century ago, American political thought staged the argument this section
enters. Walter Lippmann looked at the modern citizen and reported what he saw:
people act on "pictures in their heads" of a world that is "out of reach, out
of sight, out of mind," and the omnicompetent sovereign citizen demanded by
democratic theory is a phantom (*Public Opinion*, 1922; *The Phantom Public*,
1925). His conclusion was government by insiders, weakly monitored. John Dewey
conceded the diagnosis and refused the cure: the remedy for the public's
incapacity is not shrinking the demos but "improvement of the methods and
conditions of debate, discussion and persuasion" — better means of inquiry and
communication (*The Public and Its Problems*, 1927). For a hundred years
Lippmann held the evidence and Dewey held the hope. The claim of this section
is that machine intelligence is Dewey's answer arriving a century late — and
that seeing why requires walking the chain that made Lippmann right in the
first place.

Start with what powerful people have always had that others have not. Not
information — counsel: the standing apparatus that watches the world, models
it, and tells you what it means for what you should do. Every court funded
prophets; the point of Nathan's parable to David, or the court astrologers of
every early state, was structural risk assessment delivered to power with
protected candor. The institutional forms are a lineage — the jester licensed
to say what advisers could not, imperial China's Censorate with its standing
mandate to criticize the throne, the modern equivalents in the IPCC and the
Political Instability Task Force, whose structural models forecast state
failure two years out with better than 80 percent accuracy from four variables
(Goldstone et al., *AJPS* 2010). The office is old and continuously staffed;
only its reach has been narrow. Counsel of this kind has been the last reliably
scarce good of de facto royalty: wealth could occasionally be seized and
literacy learned, but the standing apparatus that converts the world's
complexity into wise action has never been mass-produced. [⟨M⟩ framing:
"democratization of that intelligence, which was one of the only remaining
scarce goods supporting de facto royalty."]

The republic itself is the institutional shape of that scarcity. Madison's
defense of representation is explicitly a counsel argument: pass public views
"through the medium of a chosen body of citizens" to "refine and enlarge" them
(Federalist No. 10) — delegation to those with the leisure and apparatus to
judge, because the citizen has neither. Burke made the aristocratic version
honest: the representative owes the constituent his judgment, and good judgment
is inarticulate, untransferable, resident in a natural aristocracy
(*Reflections*, 1790). Schumpeter and Manin later systematized what these
founders built — democracy as elite competition, election as aristocratic
selection (*Capitalism, Socialism and Democracy*, 1942; *The Principles of
Representative Government*, 1997). And Downs supplied the economics that makes
the arrangement an equilibrium rather than a conspiracy: for an individual
voter, the expected return on becoming informed is effectively zero, so
rational citizens stay rationally ignorant — political information is a public
good nobody's incentives provision (*An Economic Theory of Democracy*, 1957).
This is the same under-provision theorem that governs this paper everywhere
else, operating in the political domain: counsel was not withheld from the
many; it was uneconomic to provide.

Power, meanwhile, was distributed anyway — and the record says it was mostly
*forced*. Acemoglu and Robinson read the nineteenth-century franchise
extensions as elites conceding under credible threat of revolution — a
commitment device against the mob (*QJE* 2000); North and Weingast read 1688 as
sovereign power submitting to constraint because reneging had become too
expensive (*JEH* 1989). These accounts are contested in instructive ways —
Lizzeri and Persico show elites sometimes extended the franchise voluntarily to
redirect politics toward public goods (*QJE* 2004), and Ansell and Samuels find
rising groups demanding protection for new wealth (2010) — so forced
distribution is one documented channel, not a law. But the structure of the
menu is Hirschman's: pressure discharges through exit, voice, or violence
(*Exit, Voice, and Loyalty*, 1970), and history's most reliable levelers have
been the violent end of that menu (Scheidel, *The Great Leveler*, 2017 — with
Alfani's correction that peaceful compressions exist; the Black Death leveled
without revolution). The residual claim survives its qualifications: power was
distributed under pressure, ahead of the counsel needed to wield it.

That gap — power democratized before guidance — is measurable in the present
tense. Gilens and Page's estimate that average citizens exert "near-zero"
independent influence on U.S. policy across 1,779 cases (*Perspectives on
Politics*, 2014) reads, in this frame, not as a scandal but as a gauge: it is
what a counsel gap looks like from above. The finding is contested exactly as a
gauge should be — Enns shows middle-class preferences usually coincide with
elite ones ("coincidental representation," 2015), Branham, Soroka and Wlezien
find the rich win only slightly more often when preferences diverge (2017) —
and the honest sentence is that rich and middle-class preferences mostly
coincide, and the drift shows where they diverge. [Register: cite the contest
in the same breath, always.] The same gap shows up as rot at the system's
edges: institutions built on the assumption that citizens cannot evaluate
complex conduct attract conduct that exploits the assumption, and when the
blocked pressure finds no institutional channel it discharges through the
cheapest one available — Arrow's observation, made general: "given the
existence of Pareto inefficiency in a free market equilibrium, there is a
pressure in the market to overcome it by some sort of departure from the free
market" (1969). The December 2024 killing of a health-insurance executive, and
the wave of public sympathy that followed it, is this section's hypothesis in
action — voice failing, violence pricing in. [⟨M⟩: "power democratized before
that guidance… the healthcare executive that was killed. Whatever data exists
as well as that anecdote are our hypothesis in action." Register: one anecdote,
flagged as anecdote, carried by the Goldstone/PITF-style structural data around
it.]

What machine intelligence changes is the Downs constant. Rational ignorance was
never about stupidity; it was a price. When the standing apparatus that
watches, models, and advises costs effectively nothing per person, the
under-provision equilibrium that made representation-as-counsel-delegation
necessary starts to dissolve — not because citizens become omnicompetent, but
because the omnicompetence was never the requirement; counsel was. The
existence proofs have begun to publish. An LLM mediator produced consensus
statements that participants preferred to those of human mediators, at scale
(Tessler et al., *Science* 2024 — the "Habermas Machine"); a three-round
dialogue with a model durably reduced conspiracy beliefs by about 20 percent,
holding at two months — movement on exactly the beliefs the pseudo-environment
argument says are unmovable (Costello, Pennycook & Rand, *Science* 2024);
automated moderation scales structured deliberation to thousands (Fishkin et
al., 2025 — facilitation, not counsel; the two must not be conflated). The
counterweight publishes too: the same capability is a persuasion lever with
measurable purchase (Hackenburg et al., 2025), which is why the audit
architecture below is not optional. [No-novelty convention: the Downs-flip
framing appears unclaimed in the literature — four sweep seeds confirm — but
state the observation quietly and let the assembled results carry it.]

The formal tradition says what the flip is worth. Condorcet's jury theorem has
a sign: above one-half average competence, adding voters drives collective
judgment toward certainty; below it, toward error (1785). The whole realist
tradition from Lippmann forward is an argument that the electorate sits below
the threshold on most questions. Machine counsel is a lever on the competence
term itself — and the standard objection, that a shared advisor correlates
everyone's errors and voids the theorem, has a published answer: deference to a
shared opinion leader can raise group competence without destroying the
independence the theorem needs, within limits that are exactly the design
constraints (Estlund, 1994; the correlation mathematics in Dietrich &
Spiekermann, 2013). Diversity results point the same direction with their own
caveats (Hong & Page, *PNAS* 2004 — contested by Thompson, 2014; cite as
motivation, not theorem), and Landemore's epistemic case for open democracy
(*Democratic Reason*, 2013; *Open Democracy*, 2020) is the political synthesis
this section extends: her argument for inclusion is an argument about where
knowledge lives, and machine counsel moves it.

Two consequences, stated in ascending order of reach. First: democracy is
vindicated rather than replaced. The realist critique was always a critique of
the counsel gap, not of the demos — on this account, democracy was never
wrong; it was early. ⟨M⟩ Second: the scales refine. Inherited advantage has
always bundled genetic endowment with structural advantages — the tutors, the
networks, the standing apparatus. Democratized counsel strips the bundle down
to what is actually in the person: the scales come to weigh only the physical
genetic reality and virtue. ⟨M⟩ And in the limit where machine competence makes
human competence a rounding error, even the natural lottery deflates and only
virtue is left to judge — but that comes after. ⟨M⟩ [Register: [IMAGINED],
descending; the "comes after" sentence is the calibration.]

What remains for design is the office itself. The counsel that watched for
structural collapse was historically staffed by humans channeling something
larger than any individual — prophet, jester, censorate, task force. The
artifact this program builds and tests (study S15) is the continuation:
a standing four-horsemen brief that any holder of significant power runs in the
background, surfacing pressing structural risks through historical analogy and
quantitative anchor — the PITF's structural models generalized and
personalized. Its legitimacy requirement is the one this whole section
establishes: counsel earns abdication only by demonstrated wisdom, and its
audit is distributed — the many instantiations checking each other, because
the office was never safe as a monopoly. [Bridge: the household-scale twin of
this artifact is Arc 2's firm.]

## Arc 2 — The trustworthy firm

[Integration target V.3b. The proposal and several sentences are Matthew's
(2026-07-30), marked ⟨M⟩; the register ceiling of this section is the
religious-institution claim, stated as the author's. This arc assumes the
Hayek-criterion section has already taken preferences as given; here is where
preference endogeneity natively lives.]

Suppose the capability the rest of this paper argues toward: an intelligence
that knows a human's preferences — a time series of utility functions,
realized at decision time — uncertainly, but better than the human knows them
themselves. ⟨M⟩ The question is no longer whether such a thing can exist; the
behavioral-welfare literature already licenses the premise in its own terms
(choices misreveal preferences: Bernheim & Rangel, *QJE* 2009), and the
measurement record below shows the knowing is largely done. The question is
deployment: how do you field it, and its competitors, without eroding anyone's
freedom? ⟨M⟩ One answer fits inside existing market structures and requires no
change in governance. ⟨M⟩

The market inefficiency it corrects is the *internality* — the wedge between
what a person chooses and what their own preferences, honestly accounted,
would have them choose (Herrnstein, Loewenstein, Prelec & Vaughan, 1993, who
named it; Gruber & Kőszegi, *QJE* 2001, who taxed it; Allcott, Lockwood &
Taubinsky, *QJE* 2019, who priced it at 31–37 percent of consumption in their
domain and showed it falls hardest on the poor). The wedge is real, large,
per-person, and already monetized — by the wrong side. Health-club contracts
are priced against members' overestimates of their own attendance, at more
than $17 per actual visit against a $10 pay-per-visit alternative (DellaVigna
& Malmendier, *AER* 2006); credit-card rewards run a measured $15 billion a
year from naive to sophisticated cardholders, within income bands (Agarwal,
Presbitero, Silva & Wix, 2023). A firm that profits by *closing* the wedge
instead of farming it is the fair-split twin of that machinery — the same
capability under opposite governance.

The vehicle: a bank whose capital's purchasing power varies with how it is
spent. ⟨M⟩ It lends to employers at discounted or even negative rates, in
exchange for the right to communicate with employees and suggest purchases;
it profits when its recommendations raise the value of its own assets. In the
degenerate case it is an ordinary bank. ⟨M⟩ Money with conditions is not an
invention here: earmarked money is among the oldest social technologies
(Zelizer, *The Social Meaning of Money*, 1994), the economics of restricted
transfers is a settled literature (Nichols & Zeckhauser, 1982; Currie &
Gahvari, *JEL* 2008), and the labels demonstrably bind — the marginal
propensity to consume food out of SNAP benefits runs 0.5–0.6 against roughly
zero out of cash (Hastings & Shapiro, *QJE* 2018). Nor is the firm itself
unprecedented; it exists in pieces. Save More Tomorrow delivered
welfare-improving defaults through the employer channel, voluntarily adopted,
and moved savings rates at scale (Thaler & Benartzi, *JPE* 2004) — everything
but the profit-sharing loop. Real banks have profitably sold commitment: SEED
deposit-freezes in the Philippines (Ashraf, Karlan & Yin, *QJE* 2006), CARES
smoking contracts (Giné, Karlan & Zinman, 2010), and — closest in structure —
Kenyan dairy buyers supplying commitment through the payment channel itself,
priced as a negative interest rate that farmers accept, with *credibility
rather than capital* rationing who may supply it (Casaburi & Macchiavello,
*AER* 2019). A fintech that helps users hit their own stated savings goals,
profitably, is the firm in embryo (Gargano & Rossi, *J. Finance* 2024).

Why, then, doesn't the firm already exist at scale? Because the market
equilibrium selects its extractive twin — and this is a theorem, not a
grievance. When some consumers misperceive themselves, competition does not
protect them: firms shroud, and the "curse of debiasing" means the honest
competitor who educates the customer merely donates margin to the shrouder
(Gabaix & Laibson, *QJE* 2006); credit markets in equilibrium design contracts
*for* naive present bias (Heidhues & Kőszegi, *AER* 2010); and the deployed
record obliges — the flagship workplace-wellness RCT moved 37 of 39 outcomes
not at all while the vendor profited by selection (Jones, Molitor & Reif,
*QJE* 2019). The critique goes deeper than markets: optimizing on revealed
human approval selects sycophancy — convincing agreement over truth — inside
the training objective itself, before any market pressure arrives (Sharma et
al., 2023). Any proposal in this space that does not answer
Heidhues-Kőszegi-Gabaix-Laibson head-on is selling the disease as the cure.

The answer is the paper's own thesis applied locally: what changed is the cost
structure, and the evidence is asymmetric — one leg has collapsed, one is a
frontier. *Knowing the per-person wedge* has collapsed. Machine-estimated
individual willingness-to-pay is deployed and priced (Dubé & Misra, *JPE*
2023); each payday borrower's own repayment misprediction has been elicited
and priced (Allcott, Kim, Taubinsky & Zinman, *REStud* 2022); the identical
identification was possible pre-ML only through a bespoke 4,500-person survey
(Handel & Kolstad, *AER* 2015) — that survey is what a model now replaces.
Machines model the person better than the surrounding humans do: better than
judges at bail (Kleinberg et al., *QJE* 2018), better than physicians at
testing (Mullainathan & Obermeyer, *QJE* 2022), better than friends and
spouses at personality (Youyou, Kosinski & Stillwell, *PNAS* 2015), and now
one foundation model predicts individual choices out-of-distribution better
than bespoke cognitive models (Binz et al., *Nature* 2025). The claim has a
measured ceiling — mass collaboration on rich longitudinal data still could
not predict individual life outcomes well (Salganik et al., *PNAS* 2020) —
and the argument lives comfortably inside it: the wedge is not a life
trajectory; it is a local, decision-time quantity, exactly what the record
shows machines price well. And per-person is not a refinement but the
threshold of legitimacy: the *uniform* version of a beneficial intervention
harms 15 percent or more of its recipients even as it helps on average
(Knittel & Stolper, 2025). Correction that cannot see the individual is not a
gentler paternalism; it is a coin flip wearing one.

*Demonstrating wisdom legibly* — the trust leg — is the honest frontier, and
the firm's design is the engineering of it. The constraint is behavioral
before it is technical: people abandon a demonstrably superior advisor after
one visible error (Dietvorst, Simmons & Massey, 2015), yet restore adoption
when given trivial control over it (Dietvorst et al., *Mgmt Sci* 2018), and
often over-weight algorithmic advice once they take it at all (Logg, Minson &
Moore, 2019) — which cuts both ways, because confident advice gets accepted
beyond the frontier of its competence (Dell'Acqua et al., 2024) and a deployed
objective can be wrong for years before anyone can see it (an algorithm
serving 200 million people optimized cost as a proxy for need, under-serving
Black patients until outside researchers with inside access caught it —
Obermeyer et al., *Science* 2019). Trust, meanwhile, is the binding constraint
on the whole channel, formally (clients pay trusted managers even at lower
returns — Gennaioli, Shleifer & Vishny, "Money Doctors," *J. Finance* 2015)
and empirically (stock-market participation itself is gated on trust — Guiso,
Sapienza & Zingales, *J. Finance* 2008). The short-term-profit version of
persuasion is rightly seen as exploitative, so the instance of the firm that
maximally demonstrates what humans recognize as wisdom maximizes
participation. ⟨M⟩ This may make the firm, necessarily, an iteration on
religious institutions — the only social structures to which humans have
shown themselves willing to fully abdicate decision-making. ⟨M⟩ [Register
ceiling; author's claim, stated once, carried by the GSS datapoint — poor
frequent attenders report higher happiness than wealthy non-attenders —
engineered utility functions delivering more welfare per unit of material
input.]

Three independent literatures then converge on what the design must be, and
the convergence is the section's spine. Preference-trained systems drift
sycophantic (Sharma). Reputation-motivated experts distort reporting even
with zero conflict of interest, and competition does not fix it (Ottaviani &
Sørensen, 2006). Advisor harm concentrates precisely where the advisor's
compensation has no continuing exposure to the client's outcomes — front-load
funds underperform; asset-linked share classes do not (Christoffersen, Evans
& Musto, *J. Finance* 2013). Every branch of the evidence eliminates every
design except one: **the advisor's balance sheet must move with the realized
outcome of the advice.** That is what the firm's structure does mechanically
— it holds the assets whose value its recommendations move, so it profits
only when the correction is real. Formally, this is the λ that turns
equilibrium advice from cost-minimizing to welfare-tracking (Baicker,
Mullainathan & Schwartzstein, *QJE* 2015); institutionally, it is
Grossman's old result that the seller who must warranty is the seller who
tells the truth.

The limit case scales the same logic to the economy. Like the institutions it
iterates on, the firm compounds by network effects, and in the diversified
limit it profits from the most abstract notion of human well-being: a
sufficiently diversified profit maximizer benefits from any real increase in
productivity or well-being, to the extent the economy reflects it, and is
thereby incentivized to maintain the health of the markets in turn. ⟨M⟩ This
is the universal-owner thesis (Hawley & Williams, *The Rise of Fiduciary
Capitalism*, 2000) run as an alignment mechanism rather than an antitrust
worry — and it must be argued as mechanism, not practice, because the
practice record is honest and mixed: actual universal owners steward feebly
(Fichtner & Heemskerk, 2020), for identified agency-cost reasons (Bebchuk &
Hirst, 2019); the anticompetitive reading is contested in both directions
(Azar, Schmalz & Tecu, *J. Finance* 2018, against Dennis, Gerardi & Schenone,
*J. Finance* 2022); what is measured beyond dispute is the exposure shift
itself — implied internalization of rivals' profits up roughly sevenfold
since 1980 (Backus, Conlon & Sinkinson, 2021) — and the one deployed
natural experiment shows a sovereign universal owner systemically diffusing
its governance preferences through the market (Aguilera, Bermejo, Capapé &
Cuñat, 2020; Condon, 2020, states the climate version of the thesis).
Stewardship at universal-owner scale awaits exactly the intelligence this
paper supplies; that is a what-changed argument, the same one, at its widest
scope.

Three tensions stay in the text, named. The firm is one governance slip from
the thing it replaces — "suggests purchases for profit" describes both twins
— and the guards are the outcome-coupled balance sheet and the demonstration
requirement, not anyone's intentions. It concentrates exactly the counsel the
previous arc democratizes, so it exists legitimately only in the plural —
many such firms, legible, competing — or it rebuilds the moat it was designed
to drain. And its epistemics must stay disciplined: the machine does not
"simply know your utility function" — overriding a person's choice is earned
per-instance by demonstrated characterization failure, and welfare judgments
come as intervals, not points (Bernheim, 2016). The firm's product, at
bottom, is Bernheim's frame C — an advisor supplying option-consequence
information a person can act on — delivered at the only scale at which it was
ever going to be affordable: everyone's.
