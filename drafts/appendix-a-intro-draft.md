## What the program is betting on

The program does not require knowing which door opens first. Three forces press
on the same barrier, and the plan needs only one of them to arrive.

The first is already moving: as machine intelligence becomes integral to how
firms operate, they instrument, schedule and actually utilise their own endpoint
fleets — because they want the inference, not because anyone asked them to sell
capacity. The infrastructure that makes a fleet harvestable gets built for
internal reasons, and selling the surplus afterwards is a marginal step rather
than a new capability. The second is arithmetic: the cost of the security review,
the liability opinion and the policy exception that would release a fleet is
roughly fixed, while the revenue those machines could earn rises with the price
of compute. There is a level at which the diligence is obviously worth
commissioning, and the memory and accelerator markets described in §4 are moving
toward it, not away. The third is the one this program builds, and §8.2 named the
instrument: the hazard of admitting outside code to a managed estate is
insurable, and the party that built the isolation is the party that can price it.

Stated honestly, that is **one bet and one build**. The first two forces share a
driver — if demand for machine compute stalls, both weaken together — so they are
not three independent hedges, and the plan should not be sold as though they
were. The bet is the one this paper argues from beginning to end: that demand for
compute does not saturate, because machine demand has no floor. Two separate
mechanisms convert that bet into available supply without the program doing
anything at all. The build is the risk instrument, which is the piece nobody else
is making, and the only piece that is genuinely ours to get right.

## Why the household is still the first customer

§8.2 argued the household prices only its own tradeoff while an institution's
gatekeeper prices career risk, and §4 showed the institutional fleet is the
better substrate on every axis except that one. Both remain true, and the program
takes them in order rather than choosing between them. The household is where a
device-financing instrument can be offered without an indemnity, a procurement
cycle or a security review, and it is where the network can reach the scale that
makes an institutional guarantee credible in the first place. An underwriter with
no operating history cannot insure anyone.

The instrument is also no longer an unusual thing to offer. Financing a personal
computing device has become ordinary rather than exotic: [[VERIFY: carrier
equipment-instalment-plan penetration]], and point-of-sale instalment credit has
grown from a niche product to a mainstream one over the last decade [[VERIFY:
CFPB BNPL volumes / Fed G.19]]. The paper's two older precedents — third-party
financed rooftop solar at 59% of new U.S. residential installations at its 2012
peak, and the carrier-subsidised handset — established that consumers accept an
instrument whose payments are met by the asset's own output. What has changed
since is that they now expect it. The device that pays for itself with its own
idle time is a new application of a financing pattern households already use, not
a new financial habit they must be taught.

## What accumulates

The margin on any single device-hour is small, and the paper's own analysis says
it should be expected to fall: Figure 4's demand curve extends toward arbitrarily
low value precisely because machine demand keeps finding a lower-value next-best
task. A plan that depends on holding a price per device-hour is betting against
the argument that justifies it.

What accumulates instead is the network — the isolation that makes untrusted work
safe to run, the scheduler that places it, the reputation that lets capacity be
sold with a promise attached, and the installed base that has already accepted
the instrument. Those assets appreciate on exactly the trend the paper argues is
coming. If continuous optimization becomes a public good supplied by machine
intelligence, the scarce thing is not compute but the capacity to place work
across heterogeneous, untrusted, intermittent hardware and be accountable for the
result. That is the aggregation point of §7 arriving from the supply side, and it
is the same asset whether the workloads it carries are today's batch jobs or the
shared optimization commons the paper describes. **The network is the position;
the compute revenue is how it is financed while it is built.**
