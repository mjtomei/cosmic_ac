# Is partisanship an information shortcut? The evidence, both ways

Testing V.3a's first move (Matthew, 2026-08-05): that partisanship is a
heuristic for incomplete information, and a full per-situation view plus a view
of everyone around you would let people identify true allies case by case.

**Verdict: conditionally supported, with one strong empirical anchor and two
serious refutations. The claim survives only in a weaker and more interesting
form than stated.** Sweep results: `partisanship-sweep.json`. Two of four angles
failed on the schema layer (deliberation evidence; signalling) and are still
outstanding.

## The strongest support — and it is close to Matthew's exact mechanism

**Ahler & Sood, "The Parties in Our Heads," *Journal of Politics* 80(3), 2018.**
People hold wildly wrong beliefs about who is in the other party: they think 32%
of Democrats are LGBT (actual 6%) and 38% of Republicans earn over $250k (actual
2%). **Told the real composition, partisans "see its supporters as less extreme
and feel less socially distant from them."**

That is "a view of everyone around you," administered as a treatment, reducing
partisan distance. It is the best available evidence for the claim and it should
be the anchor when the argument is written.

**Druckman, Peterson & Slothuus, *APSR* 107(1), 2013** supplies the mechanism
directly: "cues become important when substantive information is inconclusive…
Only when the arguments are equally strong do they turn to party cues to find
their way." Under *low* polarization the first thing considered is the argument,
not the label. Bullock (*APSR* 2011) agrees that "party cues have an effect, but
do not overwhelm content."

## The refutations, which are stronger than I expected

**1. Positions follow allegiance, not the reverse.** Slothuus & Bisgaard, *AJPS*
65(4), 2021 — a five-wave panel around two Danish parties abruptly reversing
their welfare positions. Supporters moved about **15 points immediately, "even
when the new position went against citizens' previously held views," and up to 36
points among initial opponents.** If allegiance rewrites the position, better
information about the situation does not touch the thing doing the work.

**2. More reflection makes it worse, not better.** Kahan, *Judgment and Decision
Making* 8(4), 2013: motivated reasoning "is not a consequence of over-reliance on
heuristic or intuitive forms of reasoning. On the contrary, subjects who scored
highest in cognitive reflection were the **most** likely to display ideologically
motivated cognition." His motivated-numeracy work (2017) finds polarization
greatest among the most numerate. Every version of "give them a better view"
runs into this.

And Kahan states the alternative function precisely: motivated cognition
"promotes individuals' interests in forming and maintaining beliefs that signify
their **loyalty to important affinity groups**." **If partisanship is a loyalty
signal rather than an inference shortcut, a complete situational picture removes
no reason to hold it.** That is the sharpest form of the objection.

**3. Cue-taking tracks *apparent*, not actual, alignment.** In Lupia &
McCubbins's own experiment, principals falsely told they shared interests with a
speaker accepted the advice exactly as when interests genuinely aligned (via
Somin, *Yale J. on Regulation* 16:401, 1999). This bears directly on the
dishonest-signalling sub-claim: making *real* alignment visible only helps if
people attend to real rather than professed alignment, and this says they do not
by default.

## What the claim can honestly become

Not "information dissolves partisanship." The defensible version is narrower and
better, and Druckman supplies it:

> Cue reliance and polarization are **mutually reinforcing**. Polarization
> "intensifies the impact of party endorsements, decreases the impact of
> substantive information, and stimulates greater confidence in those — less
> substantively grounded — opinions." Reliance on the heuristic is not merely a
> response to scarcity; it is also a *product* of the division it sustains.

So the claim becomes: **partisanship is a heuristic whose reliance is endogenous
to polarization, and cheap per-situation counsel acts on one term of a feedback
loop.** That is a real mechanism, it is consistent with Ahler & Sood, and it does
not require the identity literature to be wrong — it only requires the
informational component to be non-zero at the margin, which Druckman and Bullock
establish.

It also predicts the right failure mode: the intervention should work best where
polarization is *lowest*, and worst exactly where it is most needed. That is an
uncomfortable prediction and the paper should state it rather than wait for a
reviewer to.

## Consequence for V.3a

- Lead with **Ahler & Sood**, not with the general claim.
- Carry **Kahan** and **Slothuus & Bisgaard** in the same breath — the paper's
  convention is that contested findings appear with their contest.
- Drop any phrasing implying information *dissolves* partisanship. Say it acts on
  one term of a loop, and name the term.
- The dishonest-signalling sub-claim needs the most work: Lupia & McCubbins show
  people follow *apparent* alignment, so machine-derived positions help only if
  people prefer the derivation to the profession. That is an adoption question,
  not an inference question, and it belongs with the trust material in V.3b.

## Still outstanding

Two angles failed and are worth re-running: the deliberation evidence (Fishkin's
"Is Deliberation an Antidote to Extreme Partisan Polarization?", *APSR* 115(4),
2021 — surfaced but truncated; and the Stanford Strengthening Democracy Challenge
megastudy, which tested 25+ depolarization interventions at scale), and the
interests/signalling angle (Mercier & Sperber on argumentation, Kuran on
preference falsification, and the hazards of legible preferences).
