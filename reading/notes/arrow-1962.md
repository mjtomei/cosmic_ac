# Reading notes — Arrow 1962 (read in full for S17 v2, 2026-08-06)

"Economic Welfare and the Allocation of Resources for Invention," in *The
Rate and Direction of Inventive Activity* (Universities-NBER, Princeton UP,
1962), pp. 609–626. Read against the NBER chapter scan (nber.org/chapters/
c2144). All quotes verbatim from the scan. Sibling: `arrow-1969.md`.

## The structure

Three classical failure reasons for competition in invention (p.609):
indivisibilities, inappropriability, uncertainty. The chapter runs risk
bearing (moral factor limits insurance; cost-plus as insurance) → information
as a commodity → invention as information production → competition vs
monopoly incentives (the replacement effect) → alternative organization.

## The passages that carry S17's corrected argument, by leg

**Leg 1 (the ledger).** p.619, the summary: "To sum up, we expect a free
enterprise economy to underinvest in invention and research (as compared
with an ideal) because it is risky, because the product can be appropriated
only to a limited extent, and because of increasing returns in use. This
underinvestment will be greater for more basic research." And the
underutilization twin, p.617: "In a free enterprise economy, inventive
activity is supported by using the invention to create property rights;
precisely to the extent that it is successful, there is an underutilization
of the information." Both failures at once — underinvestment AND
underutilization — is the negative-ledger claim: the selling regime loses on
both margins. Also p.617: "The first problem, then, is that in a free
enterprise economy the profitability of invention requires a nonoptimal
allocation of resources." Also p.622: "the potential social benefit always
exceeds the realized social benefit."

**Leg 2 (protections fail originators).** p.615: "no amount of legal
protection can make a thoroughly appropriable commodity of something so
intangible as information." pp.617–618: patent laws "sharply restrict the
range of appropriable information" *in the interests of enforceability* —
the crudeness is designed in.

**Leg 3 (thought outside the market).** p.623: "for optimal allocation to
invention it would be necessary for the government or some other agency not
governed by profit-and-loss criteria to finance research and invention. In
fact, of course, this has always happened to a certain extent. The bulk of
basic research has been carried on outside the industrial system" — plus
"the importance of nonpecuniary incentives." p.624: "There is really no need
for the firm to be the fundamental unit of organization in invention; there
is plenty of reason to suppose that individual talents count for a good deal
more than the firm as an organization."

**Leg 4 (the compensation channel and machine attribution).**
- The channel, named: p.617: "This [free availability] insures optimal
  utilization of the information but of course provides no incentive for
  investment in research. In an ideal socialist economy, **the reward for
  invention would be completely separated from any charge to the users of
  the information.**" (fn.5 attributes the observation about the Soviet
  system to Kaplan & Moorsteen of RAND.) Sale→compensation is on the page
  in 1962, filed under an economy nobody wants to defend — the point for
  the paper is that the *separation*, not the planner, is the design.
- The attribution system, declared impossible: p.617–618, the heat-resistant
  metal example — every user of the property should pay, every follow-on
  researcher "is using the information already discovered and should pay for
  it in some measure; and any beneficiary of his discoveries should also
  pay. One would have to have elaborate distinctions of partial property
  rights of all degrees to make the system at all tolerable. In the
  interests of the possibility of enforcement, actual patent laws sharply
  restrict the range of appropriable information…" Preceded by: "Patent laws
  would have to be **unimaginably complex and subtle** to permit such
  appropriation on a large scale." This is the leg-4 hinge: Arrow specifies
  exactly what optimal appropriation requires — graded, universal,
  per-contribution attribution — and rejects it on measurement/enforcement
  cost. Machine credit assignment is that specification becoming buildable;
  the Shapley line is its formalism.
- Alternative compensation, invited: p.624: "There is clear need for further
  study of alternative methods of compensation… some part of the contractual
  payment might depend on the degree of success in invention."

## Other verbatim anchors (verified against the scan)

- **Fundamental paradox**, p.615: "there is a fundamental paradox in the
  determination of demand for information; its value for the purchaser is
  not known until he **has** the information, but then he has in effect
  acquired it without cost." (Not "knows" — common misquote.)
- p.616: "from the standpoint of efficiently distributing an existing stock
  of information, the difficulties of appropriation are an advantage,
  provided there are no costs of transmitting information, since then
  optimal allocation calls for free distribution."
- p.618, on inputs: "Information is not only the product of inventive
  activity, it is also an input — in some sense, the major input apart from
  the talent of the inventor." Basic research "especially unlikely to be
  rewarded."
- p.620–622: the replacement effect ("The preinvention monopoly power acts
  as a strong disincentive to further innovation") — useful to the herd
  material, not S17.
