## Why this matters to the counsel arc

1. **It is a founding-logic account of counsel-as-manufactured-capacity, from
   the successor institution.** The Kennedy School exists because the Great
   Society was read as *the state lacking analytical plumbing*. That is the
   counsel-gap thesis in twentieth-century American form — and it was acted on,
   with money and institutions, which makes it evidence rather than analogy.
2. **It supplies the failure diagnosis the paper needs.** The model assumed the
   *end of ideology* — that once analysis improved, decisions would follow, because
   the disagreements were technical rather than fundamental. That assumption did
   not hold. Any argument that machine counsel democratises governance has to say
   why it does not inherit the same defect, and the honest answer is probably
   about *distribution* rather than *quality*: the Kennedy School's counsel was
   built for the state, not for the citizen, so it improved the apparatus while
   leaving the Downs asymmetry untouched.
3. **"Governing across ideological difference" is our problem too.** Teles names
   as his school's centre exactly the thing the four-horsemen artifact and the
   negotiation material in V.5 are about. Worth noting that he says almost no
   research exists on the "habits and practices of the political entrepreneurs"
   who achieve transpartisan legislation.

## The work Teles cites on corporations as artificial intelligence (~35:16)

> "In some sense, **the corporation is a form of artificial intelligence**… the
> whole point of the corporation is that you're going to divide up tasks that
> could have in an early period been in one person's head, and put them into an
> organizational structure that then has to coordinate — and then the overall
> entity of the corporation will be making decisions, and then we talk about it
> like an **artificial person**. That's an old line from law. My friend **Dan
> Davies, who wrote *The Unaccountability Machine***, has talked about this
> metaphor — that there's things to be learned from when we created an artificial
> person in the form of the corporation for how we're now going to be using
> artificial technological intelligence in the future."

**Verified handle:** Dan Davies, *The Unaccountability Machine: Why Big Systems
Make Terrible Decisions — and How the World Lost Its Mind*. Profile Books (UK)
2024; University of Chicago Press (US). ISBNs 1788169549 (Profile), 0226843084 /
0226843092 (Chicago). Confirmed via OpenLibrary.

**Why it belongs in the collection.** This is the same move as the paper's
"existing superintelligences" — firms, states and religions already run as
intelligences no individual holds — arriving from management cybernetics rather
than from computing. Davies's frame is Stafford Beer's Viable System Model and
the idea of *accountability sinks*: structures that make decisions no person
made and no person can be held to. Two consequences for us:

- It is a **published, independent statement of I.2's premise** ("humanity was
  the first world computer"), which the paper currently supports mostly by
  assertion and example. Building on it is cheaper than arguing it.
- Its cybernetic lineage runs straight into the Hayek-criterion cluster —
  Beer's Project Cybersyn is the socialist-calculation debate built in hardware,
  which is the sibling directory's subject.

**Caution:** the accountability-sink thesis cuts against us as much as for us. If
distributing cognition across an organisation reliably produces decisions nobody
is accountable for, then the paper's machine-counsel apparatus is a candidate
accountability sink, not an escape from one. That is a real objection and Davies
states it better than a critic would; the answer has to be about *legibility of
the reasoning*, which is the one thing the machine version can do that the
corporate version cannot.
